# High-intent decision scenarios

Use these playbooks when the request matches. They are decision scaffolds, not fixed questionnaires. Ask only for missing information that would materially change the recommendation; otherwise state assumptions and proceed.

## Career decision

Use for quitting, changing fields, accepting an offer, moving for work, or choosing between career paths.

1. Separate the push factors from the pull factors. A bad current situation does not prove a specific alternative is good.
2. List hard constraints: cash runway, dependents, health, location, contractual limits, and timing.
3. Compare options on future learning, income reliability, option value, manager/team quality, and recovery cost—not title alone.
4. Identify the most uncertain assumption for each option and design a small test: interviews, shadowing, freelance work, a short course with a work sample, or a negotiated trial.
5. Set a decision date and minimum evidence. Avoid endless research.
6. Include a downside plan: savings floor, return path, notice timing, and conditions that cancel the move.

## Sunk-cost review

Use when past time, money, identity, or public commitment makes stopping difficult.

1. State that past irrecoverable cost should not determine future investment, while preserving lessons and obligations that still matter.
2. Ask the forward-looking question: “If this project did not exist today, would the next unit of time or money still be the best available use?”
3. Define the project’s critical unproven assumption.
4. Design one time-boxed, budget-capped test that can produce disconfirming evidence.
5. Pre-commit to continue, pivot, pause, or stop thresholds before seeing the result.
6. Distinguish stopping the current approach from abandoning the underlying goal or identity.

## Habit design

Use for study, exercise, sleep routines, writing, or other repeated behaviors.

1. Define the smallest observable behavior; do not use identity labels as the metric.
2. Identify cue, friction, immediate reward, and the competing behavior.
3. Reduce setup cost and make the first version deliberately easy.
4. Track completion frequency before optimizing duration or intensity.
5. Add progression only after the baseline behavior is stable.
6. Review missed attempts for system friction rather than moral failure; adjust environment, timing, or scope.
7. Include a reset rule so one missed day does not become abandonment.

## Difficult conversation

Use for workplace conflict, family boundaries, expectations, credit, money, or recurring behavior.

1. Separate observable events from inferred motives or diagnoses.
2. Define the desired outcome and the minimum acceptable boundary.
3. Prepare a compact structure: observation, impact, request, and next step.
4. Anticipate likely responses and write one calm fallback for denial, deflection, anger, or delay.
5. Choose timing, medium, documentation, and escalation path proportional to the stakes.
6. Include safety planning when retaliation, coercion, abuse, or material dependence may be involved.

## Multi-option decision

Use when comparing jobs, cities, purchases, projects, schools, or strategies.

1. Remove options that violate hard constraints before scoring preferences.
2. Define 4–7 criteria in the user’s language and separate near-term needs from long-term option value.
3. Do not assign precise weights to uncertain facts. First identify which unknowns could reverse the ranking.
4. Gather targeted evidence or run small tests for those decision-sensitive unknowns.
5. Compare downside, reversibility, regret, and the cost of delaying—not expected upside alone.
6. Set a decision deadline and a tie-break rule.
